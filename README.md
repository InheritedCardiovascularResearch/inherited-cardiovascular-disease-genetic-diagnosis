# Inherited Cardiovascular Disease: Genetic Diagnosis and Variant Interpretation

## Purpose

Genetic testing can help identify the cause of an inherited cardiovascular condition, clarify relationships between genetic findings and clinical features, and guide the assessment of at-risk relatives.

Interpreting a genetic result requires more than identifying a variant in a gene associated with heart disease. The result must be evaluated alongside the patient's clinical presentation, family history, the strength of the gene-disease relationship, and the available evidence supporting the variant's classification.

This repository documents the diagnostic principles used to evaluate genetic findings in inherited cardiovascular disease. Its scope includes genetic testing strategies, variant interpretation, genotype-phenotype relationships, family screening, and the limitations of current genetic evidence.

It is an educational research resource, not a clinical diagnostic service or a substitute for professional genetic counseling.

## Research Scope

This repository focuses on the diagnostic questions that connect genetic evidence with clinical assessment:

- How genetic testing is selected for a suspected inherited cardiovascular condition.
- How pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and uncertain variants are interpreted.
- How genetic findings are evaluated alongside clinical features and family history.
- How a confirmed familial variant can inform cascade genetic testing.
- What negative and inconclusive genetic results can and cannot establish.
- How diagnostic findings relate to research into genetically defined cardiovascular conditions.

The repository does not attempt to reproduce detailed disease biology, individual gene therapy mechanisms, or current clinical trial summaries already covered in the dedicated repositories listed below.

## 1. Genetic Testing Begins With the Clinical Phenotype

The clinical phenotype includes the observable features of a condition, such as cardiac structure, cardiac function, rhythm abnormalities, symptoms, and relevant findings in other organ systems.

For suspected cardiomyopathy, the diagnostic assessment may incorporate medical and family history, electrocardiography, cardiac imaging, and other investigations appropriate to the presentation.

Genetic testing is interpreted within this clinical context. The 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend focusing initial testing on genes with robust associations with the presenting phenotype. Broader analysis may be considered when initial testing does not identify a cause and suspicion of a monogenic condition remains high.

The appropriate testing strategy depends on the clinical question, the suspected condition, and the capabilities and limitations of the test.

Reference: [2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiomyopathies](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/37/3503/7246608).

### Testing approaches

**Targeted testing** examines a known familial variant or a specific genetic finding when the clinical question is sufficiently defined.

**Multigene panels** assess a selected group of genes associated with a particular clinical presentation.

**Broader sequencing** may be considered when the suspected genetic cause remains unresolved after an appropriate initial investigation.

These approaches are not interchangeable in every clinical situation. The choice should reflect the suspected disease, the available evidence, and the limitations of the testing method.

## 2. Variant Classification and Interpretation

Genetic testing may identify variants classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variants of uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign.

These classifications describe the strength of the evidence concerning a variant's relationship with disease. They do not independently describe the severity of a person's condition or predict an individual's clinical course.

### Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants

A pathogenic or likely pathogenic classification indicates that the available evidence supports a disease-causing interpretation.

Its relevance to a patient depends on the gene-disease relationship, the inheritance pattern, the specific variant, and the consistency of the finding with the clinical presentation.

When an appropriate disease-causing variant is identified in an affected individual, targeted testing may be offered to at-risk relatives through a clinically appropriate cascade testing process.

### Variants of uncertain significance

A variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is a finding for which the available evidence does not establish whether it is disease-causing or benign.

A VUS should not automatically be treated as the explanation for a patient's condition. Clinical assessment, family segregation data, functional evidence, and future research may help clarify its significance.

Cascade testing should not be used to treat a VUS as an established familial disease-causing variant. Family studies may nevertheless be undertaken in selected circumstances to help evaluate the variant.

### Negative results

A negative genetic test means that the analysis did not identify a reportable genetic explanation within the scope of the test. It does not necessarily exclude an inherited condition.

Possible explanations include limitations in the genes or variant types assessed, technical limitations, and genetic causes that remain unknown.

When clinical suspicion remains, further clinical assessment or additional testing may be appropriate.

Reference: [ESC clinical consensus statement on integrating genetic testing into cardiomyopathy diagnostic pathways](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/4/344/7915804).

## 3. Genotype-Phenotype Relationships

Genotype-phenotype analysis examines how genetic findings relate to observable clinical characteristics.

A relationship between a gene and a disease does not mean that every person with a relevant variant will have the same clinical presentation. Individuals may differ in age at onset, disease manifestations, severity, and progression.

Important considerations include:

- **Penetrance:** whether a person with a particular genetic variant develops the associated phenotype.
- **Variable expressivity:** differences in how a condition manifests among affected individuals.
- **Inheritance pattern:** how a genetic condition is transmitted and how the relevant variant contributes to disease.
- **Clinical context:** whether the patient's features and family history support the proposed genetic explanation.
- **Evidence strength:** the quality and consistency of research supporting the variant's classification and the gene-disease relationship.

These factors help explain why genetic results must be interpreted alongside clinical evidence.

### Implications for research

Genotype-phenotype data can help researchers characterize patient groups, investigate differences in clinical presentation, and identify questions for further study.

However, an observed association does not by itself establish causation. Findings must be evaluated according to the study design, population, sample size, methods, and supporting evidence.

## 4. Family Screening and Cascade Genetic Testing

When a disease-causing variant is established in an affected individual, the result may help identify relatives who could benefit from targeted genetic testing and clinical evaluation.

Cascade testing is the process of offering appropriate testing to relatives of an individual with an established familial pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant.

A typical pathway includes:

1. Clinical evaluation of the affected individual.
2. Genetic testing when indicated.
3. Interpretation of the result in the context of the clinical and family history.
4. Genetic counseling about the implications of the finding.
5. Targeted testing and appropriate clinical assessment of at-risk relatives.

Relatives who carry the familial variant may require clinical assessment and ongoing surveillance, depending on the condition and individual circumstances.

Relatives who do not carry the established familial variant may be able to stop specific surveillance for that familial genetic condition when they have no relevant clinical phenotype. This decision must account for the clinical context, family history, symptoms, and any new evidence that could warrant reassessment.

When no disease-causing variant has been identified, clinical screening may still be indicated for relatives based on the family history and suspected condition.

Cascade genetic testing should not be based solely on a VUS as though it were an established disease-causing variant.

Reference: [2023 ESC Guidelines for the management of cardiomyopathies](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/44/37/3503/7246608).

## 5. Limitations and Unresolved Questions

Genetic diagnosis is affected by limitations in available evidence, testing technology, and the interpretation of individual findings.

### Incomplete genetic knowledge

Not all genetic causes of inherited cardiovascular disease have been identified. A negative result may therefore leave the underlying cause unresolved.

### Variant reclassification

Variant classifications may change as new clinical, population, segregation, or functional evidence becomes available. Reassessment may be appropriate when clinically relevant new information emerges.

### Population representation

Genetic databases and published studies do not represent all populations equally. Underrepresentation can limit the evidence available for interpreting variants in populations that have been studied less extensively.

### Incidental findings

Broad sequencing may identify variants in cardiovascular disease-associated genes that were not the original reason for testing. Such findings require careful interpretation and assessment of their clinical relevance rather than automatic assumptions about disease or future risk.

### Limits of prediction

A genetic result does not always predict the age at onset, severity, or progression of a cardiovascular condition. Clinical management should reflect the specific disease, the available evidence, and the individual's circumstances.

Reference: [American Heart Association scientific statement on interpreting incidentally identified variants in genes associated with heritable cardiovascular disease](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/HCG.0000000000000092).

## 6. Relevance to Cardiovascular Therapeutic Research

Genetic diagnosis can help define the population being studied in research on genetically associated cardiovascular conditions. Identifying the relevant genetic cause can clarify the biological question under investigation and help establish appropriate eligibility criteria for a clinical study.

However, diagnosis and treatment evaluation are separate processes. Identifying a disease-associated variant does not establish that a particular therapy will be safe or effective. Those questions require appropriate preclinical and clinical evidence.

The Inherited Cardiovascular Research organization maintains separate repositories for three Rocket Pharmaceuticals programs:

- **[PKP2-Associated Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy](https://github.com/InheritedCardiovascularResearch/pkp2-arrhythmogenic-cardiomyopathy):** PKP2 biology, the associated cardiac condition, and gene therapy research.
- **[Danon Disease and RP-A501](https://github.com/InheritedCardiovascularResearch/danon-disease-rp-a501):** Danon disease, LAMP2 biology, and RP-A501 development.
- **[BAG3-Associated Dilated Cardiomyopathy and RP-A701](https://github.com/InheritedCardiovascularResearch/BAG3-Associated-Dilated-Cardiomyopathy-and-RP-A701):** BAG3-associated disease and RP-A701 development.

This repository serves a different purpose. It provides a shared reference for understanding the diagnostic process and interpreting genetic evidence across inherited cardiovascular conditions, without duplicating the disease-specific material in those repositories.
